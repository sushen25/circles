# Brand strategy and naming research

_Research date: 2 September 2026_

_Product context: [initial MVP product specification](../initial-mvp-product-spec.md), [meetup market research](./meetup-market-research.md), [consumer SaaS success research](./consumer-saas-success-research.md), [software moat research](./software-moats-research.md), and [design manifesto](./design-manifesto.md)_

## Executive recommendation

Build the brand around one idea:

> **Make room for each other.**

The product should be positioned as **the considerate way for existing friends to turn good intentions into time together**. It should not present itself as another calendar, event platform, social network, party app, or cure for loneliness.

The brand needs to hold two truths at once:

- It is quietly capable software that removes scheduling and organising work.
- Its value is human: people who already care about each other spend more time together.

The recommended brand character is **a considerate friend who happens to be excellent at organising**—warm, discreet, calm, plain-spoken, and decisive when a decision is needed. The brand should feel designed for adults with full lives, not for productivity enthusiasts or a permanently online social scene.

The current working name, **Rounds**, should be replaced before public launch. A live app launched in the same broad category now uses Rounds for real-world meetups, calls each meetup a “Round,” and appears in both major app stores. This creates obvious search, app-store, word-of-mouth, and potential trademark confusion even though it currently focuses on meeting new people rather than coordinating existing friends. [Rounds competitor](https://gorounds.app/en/) and [Google Play listing](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=app.gorounds)

No alternative in this document is legally cleared. The strongest naming directions to test are:

1. **Samesoon** — distinctive and optimistic; suggests finding the same time soon.
2. **Daymade** — outcome-led and warm; the meetup made the day.
3. **Gatherkind** — directly expresses considerate gathering, though a small retail use already exists.
4. **See You Then** — exceptionally natural and emotionally right, but less ownable as a common phrase.
5. **The Next One** — reinforces recurring friendship without sounding like a calendar.

The naming recommendation is not to choose one from a document. Test the top three or four in real invite links, app-store cards, and spoken recall; then conduct formal trademark, company-name, domain, social-handle, and app-store clearance in intended launch markets.

## 1. Research scope and limitations

This work synthesises:

- the existing Rounds market, product, consumer SaaS, moat, architecture, and design research;
- current official positioning from Howbout, TimeTree, Partiful, Cupla, Rounds, ComeAlong, Gatherwell, Count Us In, Up n Around, Weavv, and adjacent products;
- current app-store and web search results for potential naming conflicts;
- evidence-based brand concepts including category entry points, mental availability, distinctive assets, emotional brand building, and trademark distinctiveness.

It answers four questions:

1. What should the brand stand for?
2. How should it be positioned against alternatives?
3. What verbal and visual themes should it explore?
4. What naming directions deserve validation?

This is strategic research, not legal clearance or representative consumer research. Search results can miss unindexed products and pending trademarks. The proposed strategy must be tested outside the founder's own groups before it is treated as market evidence.

## 2. The category brand landscape

### 2.1 Competitors occupy five main territories

| Territory | Representative brands | Typical promise | Typical expression | Strategic weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Calendar utility | Google Calendar, Apple Calendar, TimeTree | See and organise everyone's time | Grids, dates, blue/green utility palettes, completeness and control | Treats time as logistics and often confuses free time with willing social time |
| Social calendar | Howbout | Put your whole social life in one place | Energetic, irreverent, colourful, young, chatty | Broad calendar replacement; higher setup, sharing, and install expectations |
| Invitation and event energy | Partiful | Make hosting and attending feel exciting | Bold graphics, party language, spectacle, memories, social proof | Usually begins after a host has already decided to hold an event |
| Relationship management | Cupla | Protect quality time with a partner | Caring, intimate, evidence-led, couple-specific | Strongly coded for two people and expands into shared-life administration |
| Spontaneous IRL social | Rounds, ComeAlong, Count Us In, Up n Around, Weavv | Turn “who's up for it?” into real-world plans | Maps, activity cards, “real people,” “actually go,” low pressure | Often focuses on strangers, public discovery, fixed ideas, or immediate availability rather than finding a time for an existing group |

The manual incumbent is the group chat. It owns reach, familiarity, conversation, and social legitimacy. It does not need a brand campaign to be remembered; it is already where the group exists.

### 2.2 Current category language is converging

Competitor messaging increasingly uses the same phrases:

- “plans that don't die in the group chat”;
- “from ‘we should hang out’ to real plans”;
- “actually meet” or “actually go”;
- “less planning, more connection”;
- “no pressure”;
- “real people, real life”;
- “make time for what matters”;
- “no app required.”

Examples are now explicit:

- Howbout promises “find the gap and lock it in” and “plans that don't die in the group chat.” [Howbout](https://howbout.app/about)
- Partiful says it moves people from “we should hang out” to memorable nights and that gathering is what gives life meaning. [Partiful](https://partiful.com/about)
- TimeTree's vision is to help people ask loved ones “Shall We?” and cultivate time together. [TimeTree](https://timetreeapp.com/intl/en/about)
- Cupla uses “less admin, more us” and positions itself around protecting quality time. [Cupla](https://www.cupla.app/about-us/)
- ComeAlong promises private circles, no guilt, no app for guests, and relief from group-chat back-and-forth. [ComeAlong](https://comealongapp.com/)
- Count Us In calls itself social life “without the pressure of a shared calendar.” [Count Us In](https://countusin.app/index.html)
- Weavv says plans die from politeness rather than logistics and privately reveals mutual interest. [Weavv](https://www.weavv.io/)

These similarities validate the problem, but they make generic category copy weak as a distinctive brand asset. Phrases such as “real plans” and “less planning, more connection” can explain the category, but they cannot carry this brand by themselves.

### 2.3 The available whitespace

The best opening is the intersection of:

```text
Adult warmth
× social sensitivity
× quiet competence
× existing relationships
× completed meetup outcomes
```

No leading brand clearly owns **considerate coordination for existing friend groups**. The product can distinguish itself by caring about the interpersonal cost of organising:

- nobody should need to expose their calendar;
- a blank calendar is not treated as consent;
- nobody should be publicly blamed for not responding;
- mutual interest can emerge without visible rejection;
- quorum makes a practical plan possible without pretending everyone can attend;
- the product ends when people are together rather than demanding more screen time.

This is more specific than “connection” and more human than “scheduling.” It also aligns with the most defensible brand opportunity identified in the moat research: earned trust around sensitive, recurring group decisions.

## 3. Audience and human tension

### 3.1 The primary buyer and initiator

The first brand audience is the **relationship organiser** in an existing local friend group, typically aged 25–40. They care enough to initiate, but resent becoming the unpaid coordinator.

They are not searching for a more powerful calendar. They want relief from:

- asking the same question repeatedly;
- decoding ambiguous replies;
- chasing the last person;
- choosing who gets left out;
- feeling as though they care more than everyone else;
- watching sincere intentions dissolve into chat history.

### 3.2 The invited participant also judges the brand

Consumer collaborative software has two simultaneous audiences. The organiser selects the tool, but every invited friend decides whether it is welcome.

The passive participant wants:

- to understand the request immediately;
- to answer without an account or installation;
- to protect private time and calendar details;
- to decline or stay silent without feeling publicly judged;
- to trust that a response will lead somewhere;
- not to receive a new stream of promotional notifications.

The brand must therefore be desirable to the organiser and non-threatening to everyone else. Aggressive growth language, forced activation, or organiser-centric surveillance would damage the whole-group adoption loop.

### 3.3 The emotional truth

The credible human truth is:

> **People can care about each other and still fail to make plans.**

The enemy is accumulated friction, not uncaring friends. This distinction protects the brand from guilt and from making an unsupported loneliness claim.

### 3.4 What the brand must never imply

- That users are lonely, deficient, neglectful, or bad friends.
- That every friendship should be maximised or measured.
- That an empty calendar belongs to other people.
- That a failed plan reveals weak relationships.
- That more app engagement means more connection.
- That the product can manufacture relationships where willingness does not exist.

The responsible claim is narrow: the product reduces coordination friction among people who already want to spend time together.

## 4. Recommended positioning

### 4.1 Market frame

Use **friend-group planning** or **meetup planning for existing friends** as the category descriptor during early growth.

Do not lead with:

- **Social calendar:** this puts the brand into Howbout and TimeTree's strongest frame and implies shared calendar visibility.
- **Scheduling software:** accurate but cold, work-coded, and incomplete.
- **Event platform:** implies hosting, ticketing, public discovery, or a date already chosen.
- **Social network:** creates expectations for profiles, feeds, chat, content, and strangers.
- **Relationship app:** emotionally relevant but currently strongly associated with couples, therapy, and relationship management.
- **Loneliness app:** stigmatising, overpromising, and disconnected from the actual mechanism.

The descriptor can become less prominent after the brand develops recognition. Early on, clarity matters more than inventing a new category name.

### 4.2 Internal positioning statement

> For existing friend groups whose catch-ups keep getting lost in busy lives and group-chat back-and-forth, **[brand]** is a private friend-group planner that finds times people are actually willing to meet and helps the group confirm a best-enough plan. Unlike shared calendars and availability polls, it requires no app for guests, keeps personal calendars private, handles incomplete participation considerately, and stays with the circle until the meetup happens.

### 4.3 Consumer-facing promise hierarchy

| Layer | Message | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brand idea | **Make room for each other.** | Emotional memory and long-term brand platform |
| Product promise | **Turn “we should catch up” into a plan everyone can act on.** | Explains the outcome |
| Functional explanation | **Find a time your group is actually up for—without chasing everyone or sharing calendars.** | Explains how it helps |
| Proof | **No app needed to answer. Your calendar stays private. A person always confirms the plan.** | Removes adoption and trust objections |
| Action | **Plan a catch-up** | Concrete, warm CTA |

### 4.4 Why “Make room for each other” works

It connects several truths without describing a feature:

- Making room means finding time in a full life.
- It recognises that willingness and care matter more than a free slot.
- It feels reciprocal rather than organiser-led.
- It can stretch across friends, couples, families, and communities later.
- It supports visual territory around space, a place at the table, and shapes coming together.
- It is warm without claiming to fix loneliness.

It is a brand platform or campaign line, not necessarily a legally ownable tagline. It should appear consistently beside the brand name until research shows that people associate it with the product.

### 4.5 Positioning axes

The intended market position is:

| Axis | Move away from | Move toward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product frame | Calendar management | Relationship coordination |
| Human frame | Finding empty time | Making willing time |
| Decision model | Perfect unanimity | Transparent best-enough quorum |
| Social dynamic | Chasing and public rejection | Quiet, considerate participation |
| Audience | Strangers and public discovery | People who already know each other |
| Tone | Productivity or party hype | Warm adult competence |
| Data posture | More visibility and centralisation | Minimum necessary disclosure |
| Success | App activity or events created | Meetups reported as happened |

## 5. Brand platform

### 5.1 Purpose

> Help people make room for the relationships they already value.

This is an organising purpose, not an advertising claim that the software improves mental health.

### 5.2 Vision

> A world where “we should catch up” is more often followed by a date everyone knows is real.

### 5.3 Mission

> Remove the logistical and social friction that stops willing people from spending time together.

### 5.4 Values expressed as behaviours

| Value | What it means in the product and brand |
| --- | --- |
| Consideration | Never make one person's convenience another person's pressure |
| Honesty | Explain uncertainty, trade-offs, privacy, and who can attend |
| Restraint | Ask for less data, less time, and less attention |
| Usefulness | Move the group toward one real decision |
| Warmth | Reserve delight for human outcomes, not form completion |
| Agency | Recommend clearly; let a person decide |

### 5.5 Personality

The brand is:

- considerate, not tentative;
- capable, not corporate;
- warm, not saccharine;
- sociable, not performative;
- optimistic, not chirpy;
- private, not secretive;
- lightly playful, not chaotic;
- adult, not formal.

If represented as a person, it is the friend who proposes two workable dates, checks nobody is being put on the spot, books the table, and then puts their phone away.

## 6. Themes to explore

### 6.1 Primary theme: making room

This should be the dominant territory.

Possible expressions:

- Make room for each other.
- Full lives can still fit good people.
- A little room for the people who matter.
- Find the room in everyone's week.
- Your calendar can be full. Your friendships do not have to disappear.

Visual possibilities:

- an open place becoming part of a group;
- shapes shifting gently to create shared negative space;
- ordinary tables, benches, kitchens, walks, and neighbourhood places;
- generous margins and uncluttered compositions that literally create room;
- dates treated as anticipated moments, not spreadsheet coordinates.

### 6.2 Supporting theme: good intentions, made real

This is the outcome territory.

Possible expressions:

- Good intentions. Real plans.
- From “sometime” to Saturday.
- Give “we should” a when.
- The catch-up that makes it out of the chat.
- Plans people can actually act on.

Use sparingly because “real plans” and “actually” are already heavily used in the category. The stronger distinctive construction is **“From ‘sometime’ to Saturday”**, which turns vagueness into a concrete social moment.

### 6.3 Supporting theme: the kind way to organise

This is the social-safety territory.

Possible expressions:

- A kinder way to get everyone together.
- Find a time without putting anyone on the spot.
- No chasing. No calendar exposure. No hard feelings.
- Honest answers, handled thoughtfully.
- Quiet when it should be. Clear when it matters.

“Kind” should be demonstrated through behaviour and proof, not repeated until it becomes self-congratulation.

### 6.4 Supporting theme: ordinary togetherness

Partiful strongly owns the memorable party. Rounds should celebrate the smaller, recurring moments that sustain adult friendships:

- dumplings after work;
- coffee and a walk;
- the monthly dinner that keeps slipping;
- a quiet Sunday afternoon;
- a last-minute drink when several people are genuinely willing;
- the friends who do not need an occasion.

This gives the brand emotional range without spectacle. Photography should feel observed rather than staged and should include varied ages, abilities, group sizes, cultures, budgets, and types of gathering.

### 6.5 Supporting theme: rhythm without judgement

The persistent circle and cadence create repeat value, but the brand must not turn friendship into a streak.

Possible expressions:

- Whenever works. Again when it feels right.
- Keep a good thing going.
- Make the next one easier.
- Your group's rhythm, not another routine.

Avoid “stay on track,” red overdue states, declining relationship scores, and “you have not seen them in 63 days.” Cadence should invite rather than audit.

### 6.6 Themes to avoid

| Territory | Why to avoid it |
| --- | --- |
| Loneliness and mental-health rescue | Overstates the mechanism and risks shame |
| Productivity optimisation | Makes friends feel like tasks and competes with work schedulers |
| Party chaos and FOMO | Crowded by Partiful/Howbout and mismatched with ordinary recurring friendship |
| “Your whole social life” | Requires breadth the product does not need and encourages feature bloat |
| Perfect scheduling or everyone available | Impossible promise; conflicts with quorum and uncertainty |
| Social scoring and attendance performance | Turns relationships into judgement |
| Calendar transparency | Conflicts with minimum disclosure and willing-time differentiation |
| AI magic | Easy to copy, obscures human confirmation, and shifts focus from trust |
| Anti-group-chat hostility | The group chat is a useful partner and distribution surface, not an enemy to replace |
| Nostalgia as the main idea | Memories happen after the core job and are already strong competitor territory |

## 7. Verbal identity

### 7.1 Voice

The design manifesto's voice remains correct: **a considerate friend who is good at organising, not software and not a brand**.

Four practical voice traits:

1. **Plain:** “Send my times,” not “Submit availability.”
2. **Specific:** “Replies close Tuesday at 6 pm,” not “Act soon.”
3. **Warm:** “Thursday works for most of you,” not “Quorum achieved.”
4. **Unpressured:** “Not this time,” not “Decline”; “Not now,” not “Skip.”

The brand can use a slightly Australian cadence in its home market—“catch up,” “keen,” “lock it in”—but should not depend on slang that becomes confusing or performative globally.

### 7.2 Message rules

- Lead with people or the plan, not the software.
- Use “catch up,” “meet,” and “get together” more often than “event.”
- Use “time” as something people make or share, not an asset the app optimises.
- Call the persistent group a **circle** in-product, but do not rely on “circle” as a public category claim.
- Describe calendar use as an optional private check, never calendar sharing.
- State what friends will see at the moment the user provides information.
- Say when the group needs a decision; disappear when it does not.
- Never congratulate a user for supplying data or opening the app.

### 7.3 Copy examples

#### Landing page

> **Make room for each other.**
>
> Find a time your friends are actually up for—without chasing everyone or sharing calendars.
>
> **Plan a catch-up**

Proof line:

> Friends can answer from the link. No account or app needed.

#### Invite preview

> **Sunday Crew is finding a time to catch up.**
>
> Pick the times you'd actually be up for. It takes about a minute, and nobody sees your calendar.

CTA:

> **Choose my times**

#### Candidate result

> **Thursday looks good for five of you.**
>
> Priya cannot make this one. Alex has not answered yet.

CTA:

> **Review Thursday**

#### Confirmation

> **Thursday is on.**
>
> Dinner, 7:00 pm at Capitano. See you there.

#### No quorum

> **There was not enough overlap this time.**
>
> Try a wider window when the group is ready.

#### Quiet spark

> **A few people are up for getting together this weekend.**
>
> Want to find a time?

The product should not claim that “everyone wants to meet” when only a threshold has been reached.

## 8. Visual brand direction

The [design manifesto](./design-manifesto.md) already establishes an appropriate product system: warm off-white, terracotta, near-black, restrained green, Newsreader for human moments, Figtree for utility, calm working screens, and a generous confirmation moment.

Brand development should extend it rather than replace it.

### 8.1 Recommended visual territory

> **Editorial warmth with quiet utility.**

The system should feel like a well-made invitation crossed with a calm tool—not a corporate calendar and not a digital party flyer.

Use:

- warm, low-saturation colour with terracotta as the recognisable anchor;
- generous negative space as an expression of “making room”;
- human-scale typography and date-led editorial compositions;
- candid imagery of ordinary time together;
- tactile materials such as paper, menus, napkins, ticket stubs, ceramics, and neighbourhood signage, used lightly;
- an outcome-only moment of deeper colour and celebration;
- motion that closes a gap or brings separate elements into a shared arrangement.

Avoid:

- generic blue calendar UI;
- rainbow availability grids;
- neon gradients and maximalist party graphics;
- floating 3D emojis or stock “friends cheering” illustrations;
- literal loneliness imagery such as a person sitting alone;
- clocks, calendar pages, checkmarks, chat bubbles, or location pins as the main logo;
- interconnected-node graphics that make the product look like enterprise networking software;
- rings as the sole distinctive device while the current Rounds name collision remains unresolved.

### 8.2 Candidate distinctive assets

Brand research distinguishes between merely having design elements and building assets that are both unique to and widely recognised as the brand. [Ehrenberg-Bass on distinctive assets](https://marketingscience.info/brands-of-distinction/) A new brand begins with no famous assets, so it should choose a small number and use them consistently.

Recommended starting assets:

1. **Terracotta on warm ground:** retain the current palette and make the combination consistent across product, links, store assets, and communication.
2. **The made-room gesture:** two or more forms shift apart just enough to create a shared central space. This can become the logo motion, selection transition, and campaign layout.
3. **Editorial date treatment:** use Newsreader dates as the emotional centre of confirmation and invitation assets.
4. **“Make room for each other”:** consistently connect the line to the name during the brand-building period.

Do not launch with a large library of changing visual motifs. Ehrenberg-Bass recommends prioritising a small number of assets and avoiding elements already strongly associated with competitors. [Distinctive asset guidance](https://marketingscience.info/the-four-commandments-future-proofing-a-brands-identity/jenni-article-image/)

### 8.3 Photography direction

Show:

- people already comfortable with one another;
- the moment just before, during, or just after an ordinary gathering;
- varied low-cost and home-based meetups as well as hospitality venues;
- lived-in environments and imperfect arrangements;
- attention directed toward one another, not toward a phone;
- enough empty space for the brand composition to breathe.

Do not show the app as the centre of the social moment. The strongest product image is often a phone being put away after the plan is confirmed.

## 9. Mental availability and category entry points

Brand growth requires being recalled in the situations that prompt category use. Ehrenberg-Bass calls these situations **category entry points** and advises linking the brand consistently to relevant buying or usage cues. [Ehrenberg-Bass](https://marketingscience.info/learn-with-us/commercial-research/identifying-and-prioritising-category-entry-points)

Rounds should initially build memory around a focused set:

| Category entry point | User thought | Brand response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The group says “we should catch up” | “This will disappear unless somebody acts” | Start a light plan immediately |
| Nobody can find a date in chat | “There must be some overlap” | Collect willing time without a spreadsheet |
| The organiser is tired of chasing | “I do not want to be the unpaid coordinator again” | Handle responses, reminders, and trade-offs considerately |
| Everyone seems busy | “Maybe nobody actually has room” | Separate calendar conflicts from willingness and surface best-enough options |
| Someone wants to initiate without rejection | “I want to know if others are keen first” | Quiet mutual-interest threshold |
| The group has drifted from its normal rhythm | “It has been a while, but I do not want guilt” | Gentle invitation to plan the next one |
| A guest receives a plan link | “Do I have to install another app or expose my calendar?” | Answer in a minute, no installation, clear privacy proof |

The first four should dominate launch communication. Quiet sparks and cadence should enter the brand only after those behaviours work reliably.

## 10. Naming strategy

### 10.1 Naming criteria

A good name should:

- feel natural when received in a friend-group invite;
- be easy to hear, pronounce, spell, and remember across English-speaking markets;
- carry warmth without sounding like therapy or dating;
- suggest togetherness, time, recurrence, or making something happen without literally describing a calendar;
- work for both organisers and invited friends;
- stretch from friends to couples and communities if the product later expands;
- avoid a youth-only, party-only, or Australia-only meaning;
- be sufficiently distinctive for search, app stores, domains, and trademark protection;
- support a simple app icon and spoken recommendation;
- avoid negative interpretations around exclusion, monitoring, obligation, or social scoring.

WIPO advises avoiding descriptive terms as trademarks because inherently distinctive marks are generally easier to protect; descriptive names may need to acquire distinctiveness through use. [WIPO, _Making a Mark_](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/pubdocs/en/wipo-pub-900-1-en-making-a-mark-an-introduction-to-trademarks-for-small-and-medium-sized-enterprises.pdf)

### 10.2 Assessment of “Rounds”

#### What works

- Suggests recurrence: another round, the next round.
- Suggests a group arranged around something.
- Feels social and familiar.
- Works across drinks, dinner, activities, and recurring cadence.
- Is short and easy to say.

#### What does not

- A directly adjacent 2026 social-meetup app already owns the same app name and uses “Round” as its event object.
- It has substantial unrelated competition in golf, healthcare, inspections, timers, and previous social software.
- Search intent is diffuse.
- The current circular visual territory could be confused with the live competitor.
- “Rounds” can over-index on alcohol and may not travel equally across cultures or meetup types.

#### Decision

> **Keep Rounds as an internal codename only while a replacement is validated. Do not invest further in public awareness, domains, app-store optimisation, or distinctive assets under it.**

This is a brand-risk recommendation, not a legal opinion. Formal counsel must assess actual trademark classes, territories, priority dates, and likelihood of confusion.

### 10.3 Naming territories

#### Territory A: shared time

Signals that people find one workable moment together.

- **Samesoon**
- Same Time
- Some Time
- When We Can
- Together, Then
- See You Then

Strength: closely connected to the product job. Risk: ordinary time phrases can be hard to own.

#### Territory B: the meetup happened

Signals movement from intention to outcome.

- **Daymade**
- Made Time
- This One's On
- It Happened
- Be There
- The Next One

Strength: differentiates from tools that end at a poll. Risk: some options sound like campaign lines rather than company names.

#### Territory C: considerate gathering

Signals warmth and the social-safety proposition.

- **Gatherkind**
- Meet Kindly
- Good Company
- Make Room
- Among Friends
- Common Time

Strength: emotionally aligned and extensible. Risk: the vocabulary is widely used by community, wellness, hospitality, and event brands.

#### Territory D: recurring friendship

Signals the persistent circle and next meetup.

- The Next One
- Once Again
- Next Time
- Regulars
- Good Rhythm
- Still Here

Strength: supports retention and the circle moat. Risk: “next time” and “another time” can sound like postponement or rejection.

### 10.5 Evaluation of the leading candidates

#### Samesoon

**Story:** a group finds the same moment, soon enough that good intentions do not disappear.

**Strengths:** coined but understandable, warm, not limited to calendars or parties, visually supports convergence and shared space, relatively clean preliminary search.

**Risks:** some people may parse it as “same soon,” may not know whether it is pronounced as two words, and it does not immediately signal friends. It needs a clear descriptor: **Samesoon — plans with friends**.

Invite test:

> Sunday Crew is finding a time on Samesoon.

#### Daymade

**Story:** time with people can make an ordinary day; the product helps that day become real.

**Strengths:** emotionally rewarding, outcome-oriented, compact, supports “day made” language and warm creative work.

**Risks:** could be interpreted as a journaling, gratitude, photo, travel, or daily-planning app. The connection to group coordination needs consistent explanation.

Invite test:

> Sunday Crew is finding a time on Daymade.

#### Gatherkind

**Story:** both “gather kind people” and “gather kindly.”

**Strengths:** highly aligned with the considerate positioning, extensible to communities and couples, distinctive construction, easy to pronounce.

**Risks:** slightly earnest; “kind” can feel self-conscious if overused; an unrelated Gatherkind retail brand appears in search; “gather” is crowded throughout community software.

Invite test:

> Sunday Crew is finding a time on Gatherkind.

#### See You Then

**Story:** the phrase people say when uncertainty becomes a real plan.

**Strengths:** perfectly captures the desired end state, sounds human rather than technical, warm across many relationship types, highly usable in confirmation creative.

**Risks:** three words, common phrase, difficult search and trademark territory, and less natural as a verb or compact app icon. It may be strongest as the confirmation sign-off even if it is not the company name.

Invite test:

> Sunday Crew is finding a time with See You Then.

#### The Next One

**Story:** this is not an event tool; it makes the group's next ordinary meetup easier.

**Strengths:** recurring, optimistic, casual, extensible, and naturally tied to post-meetup retention.

**Risks:** does not signal scheduling, could refer to media or queues, and needs deeper collision research.

Invite test:

> Sunday Crew is finding a time for the next one.

The fact that the final test sentence works better without treating “The Next One” as a product name suggests it may also be stronger campaign language than a name.

### 10.6 Naming recommendation

Take **Samesoon, Daymade, Gatherkind, and See You Then** into structured testing. Do not include Rounds as an equal option; the purpose is to find a viable replacement, not measure attachment to the existing codename.

If one name must be used as a temporary public-facing prototype label before testing, use **Samesoon** with the descriptor **Plans with friends**. It currently has the best balance of relevance and preliminary distinctiveness, but it is not approved for launch.

## 11. Naming validation plan

### 11.1 Consumer testing

Test 12–20 people across organisers and passive participants, including people outside the founder's social circle. Show no more than four names per person and rotate order.

Do not ask only “Which do you like?” Use tasks:

1. **Five-second app-store test:** show name, icon placeholder, and descriptor; ask what the product does and who it is for.
2. **Invite test:** show “Sunday Crew is finding a time on [name]”; ask how the link feels and whether they would tap it.
3. **Spoken recall:** say the name once; ask the person to repeat and spell it later.
4. **Association test:** ask for three words, likely audience, and any negative or dating/therapy/work connotations.
5. **Memory test:** after discussing other topics, ask which names they remember unaided.
6. **Referral test:** ask how they would tell another friend about it.
7. **International comprehension:** include speakers from the likely initial global markets.

Record comprehension and recall before preference. A name people “like” but cannot remember, spell, or place in the category is weak.

### 11.2 Clearance sequence

For the final two candidates:

1. Search Apple App Store and Google Play across priority countries.
2. Search ordinary web results, company registers, startup databases, and major social platforms.
3. Check relevant domains and common misspellings; a perfect `.com` is useful but not mandatory.
4. Search IP Australia's TM Checker and databases in initial operating markets.
5. Ask a trademark professional to assess relevant goods/services classes and confusing similarity.
6. Check translation, pronunciation, slang, and adverse meanings in priority languages.
7. Secure the selected domain and handles before announcement.
8. Register the word mark where commercially justified.

Do not change application code, store metadata, or visual assets at scale until this sequence is sufficiently advanced.

## 12. Brand experience across the product loop

The brand is not only the landing page. Its most important expressions are behavioural.

| Moment | Required brand behaviour |
| --- | --- |
| First invitation | Immediate clarity, no install pressure, visible privacy reassurance |
| Availability | First-person willingness language and fast manual parity |
| Calendar permission | Exact explanation of what stays on device and what friends see |
| Waiting | No public blame, false urgency, or noisy activity alerts |
| Candidate choice | Honest trade-offs and human authority |
| Quiet spark | Protection from visible rejection and no identity leakage |
| Confirmation | Warmth, anticipation, and clear details |
| Event update email | Operational usefulness; optional email is not silently repurposed for marketing |
| Failed attempt | Neutral, forward-looking, and free of relationship judgement |
| Post-meetup | Ask whether it happened; do not demand content or performative sharing |
| Return | Make the next meetup easier without turning cadence into a score |

If advertising says “considerate” but the product pressures a guest to register, the real brand is pressure. If the privacy page is excellent but a notification exposes quiet interest, the real brand is unsafe. The moat research is correct that trust must be accumulated through product behaviour.

## 13. Launch messaging recommendations

### 13.1 App-store structure

**Name:** `[selected name]`

**Subtitle:** `Plans with friends`

**Opening:**

> Find a time your group is actually up for. Friends answer from a link, personal calendars stay private, and the best options rise without waiting for perfect overlap.

**Three proof points:**

- No app needed to answer.
- Willing time, not just empty calendars.
- Clear best-enough options; a person confirms.

Avoid leading the listing with calendar integration, AI, or feature volume.

### 13.2 Landing-page structure

1. **Make room for each other.**
2. One-sentence functional explanation.
3. **Plan a catch-up** CTA.
4. Visual demonstration of create → answer → choose → confirmed.
5. No-install and privacy proof.
6. The social problem: good intentions can disappear without anyone being at fault.
7. Ordinary meetup examples.
8. Clear beta scope and invitation.

### 13.3 Founder story

The founder story is credible because the initial segment is the founder's own lived problem:

> We liked each other. We wanted to catch up. We even said so. But full calendars, slow replies, and nobody wanting to chase meant months could pass. We built this to make the next one easier—without asking friends to share their lives with another app.

Keep the story specific and modest. Do not convert the founder's experience into a universal claim about loneliness.

## 14. Brand validation metrics

Early brand research should measure behaviour and memory, not aesthetic approval alone.

| Question | Measure |
| --- | --- |
| Is the category understood? | Unaided description after a five-second exposure |
| Is the audience understood? | Percentage identifying existing friend groups rather than dating, public meetups, or work |
| Is the promise remembered? | Unaided recall of finding a time / making the meetup happen |
| Is the brand trusted? | Comfort opening the invite, answering manually, and reading the calendar explanation |
| Is the name usable? | Pronunciation, spelling, delayed recall, adverse associations |
| Does the brand help acquisition? | Invite open-to-response and guest-to-new-circle activation by brand variant |
| Does it distinguish? | Confusion with Howbout, Partiful, Rounds, calendars, or public meetup apps |
| Does it feel considerate? | Qualitative response to no-quorum, reminder, and quiet-spark messages |

The brand has begun working when people independently describe it as the calm or considerate way their group gets a real date—not when they merely recognise the colour palette.

## 15. Decisions and next actions

### Decisions to adopt now

- Position the product around considerate friend-group planning, not shared calendars.
- Use **Make room for each other** as the leading brand platform for testing.
- Keep the existing warm, restrained design system.
- Treat ordinary togetherness as the emotional subject.
- Make privacy and no-install participation visible proof, not fine print.
- Begin replacement-name validation and stop building public equity in Rounds.

### Decisions that require research

- Final product/company name.
- Whether “Make room for each other” is understood and distinctive in live tests.
- Whether “considerate” resonates in customer language or should remain an internal design principle.
- Which category descriptor performs best: “plans with friends,” “friend-group planner,” or “meetup planner for friends.”
- Whether the terracotta/Newsreader identity remains distinctive in an app-store comparison test.

### Recommended immediate exercise

Create four otherwise identical prototype artefacts for Samesoon, Daymade, Gatherkind, and See You Then:

- one app-store card;
- one shared invite preview;
- one landing-page hero;
- one confirmation screen.

Run the comprehension, invite, spelling, association, delayed-recall, and referral tests before commissioning a finished logo or renaming the repository.
